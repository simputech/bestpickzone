Agatha Christie vs. Arthur Conan Doyle: Which Should You Read First?

By BestPickZone Books Editors · Research checked October 17, 2026

Affiliate disclosure: As an Amazon Associate, BestPickZone earns from qualifying purchases. This guide contains affiliate links.

Start with Christie for a novel built around suspects and a carefully controlled reveal; start with Doyle's Holmes stories for short cases and the pleasure of following a distinctive detective partnership. Our first picks are The Mysterious Affair at Styles and The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes. Choose The Hound of the Baskervilles if you want Doyle at novel length.

That is a reading recommendation, not a claim that one author wins literature on points. Sales totals, estimated wealth and the number of screen adaptations do not tell you which book will suit tonight's reading. The useful differences are form, atmosphere, how the investigation unfolds and how much continuity you want before opening the first page.

Which kind of mystery do you want?

For a reader who enjoys keeping a suspect list and reconsidering motives, our Christie starting points offer a sustained puzzle. For a reader who wants a complete case between other books, Doyle's Adventures offers twelve separate stories. Neither choice requires you to buy an author's complete works or commit to a long reading schedule.

Start with The Mysterious Affair at Styles for Poirot

Christie's official story page identifies Styles as her first novel, published in 1920, and Poirot's debut. Hastings visits Styles Court while recovering from a wartime injury; a poisoning brings Poirot into the household's affairs. That gives a new reader an introduction to both the detective and the kind of domestic suspicion the novel explores.

Our reason to choose it is straightforward: you can begin with the character's introduction without treating publication order as homework. Read for the interaction between what the household says and what Poirot notices. If the attraction is gathering clues across a whole novel, this is a more useful first purchase than a large omnibus selected simply because it contains more pages.

Skip this as your first pick if you want a short, self-contained session before deciding whether classic detective fiction suits you. Doyle's collection gives you that smaller commitment. If you choose Styles, confirm that the listing is the complete novel, not a simplified reader, adaptation or study guide with a similar cover.

Start with The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes for short cases

The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes collects twelve cases, including “A Scandal in Bohemia,” “The Red-Headed League” and “The Adventure of the Speckled Band.” The original text is available through Project Gutenberg. Its opening immediately places the reader with Watson observing Holmes, so you can sample the narrative voice before buying a print copy.

Our recommendation is to try the opening story, then a second case, rather than judging the entire collection from a modern adaptation. The pleasure includes Watson's reactions and Holmes's demonstrations, not just the answer to a puzzle. A collection also lets you pause between cases without abandoning a novel midway through its investigation.

Skip it if you specifically want one plot that builds across an entire book. Choose Hound instead. For print, check the contents: a similarly titled “selected adventures” edition may not contain all twelve stories. A complete Holmes collection is a different purchase again and may duplicate this volume if you later buy both.

Choose The Hound of the Baskervilles for a longer Holmes mystery

The Hound of the Baskervilles is our Doyle choice for readers drawn to an ominous setting and a sustained investigation. The novel's text sets a family legend against the work of investigating a death and threats surrounding an inheritance. It offers a different rhythm from finishing a separate case each evening.

Read this when you want the landscape and uncertainty to occupy more space. You do not need to buy the earlier Holmes books to understand the central mystery, although returning readers will already know the partnership. Our preference for a first novel is based on that immediate premise and atmosphere, not on a numerical ranking of Doyle's prose.

Skip it for now if you want Holmes and Watson in a brief London case. The collection above is better aligned with that request. Choose an unabridged edition if you want Doyle's original novel; a graphic adaptation or children's retelling can be a worthwhile separate format, but should be bought intentionally.

Choose And Then There Were None for an isolated-group mystery

Christie's official synopsis describes ten strangers invited to an island, accused of past crimes and cut off as deaths begin. Published in 1939, the novel is a standalone choice here rather than a Poirot or Miss Marple entry. That absence of a reassuring recurring investigator changes the reading experience.

This is our Christie pick for someone whose interest is mounting suspicion within a closed group. The premise asks you to watch how the surviving characters assess one another as their situation changes. Approach it without looking up the ending. Knowing the broad setup is enough to decide whether the escalating-threat structure appeals.

Skip it if your main reason for choosing Christie is to meet Poirot. Start with Styles instead. Also check that you are buying the novel: the official site discusses stage versions with different treatment of the ending. A play script is not an interchangeable edition of the prose book.

Save The Murder of Roger Ackroyd for a spoiler-free read

The official page for The Murder of Roger Ackroyd identifies a 1926 Poirot novel involving secrets, blackmail and a killing before a crucial message can be fully read. That is enough setup. Much of its reputation concerns its reveal, and a buying guide should not explain that reveal in the act of recommending the book.

Choose it when you want a complete Christie novel and can resist reading plot discussions first. A scholarly introduction may be more enjoyable after the story if it assumes familiarity with the solution. You can read this without completing every preceding Poirot title; our suggestion to begin with Styles is a convenient entry point, not a prerequisite.

Skip spoiler-heavy summaries before reading. For this book especially, an edition's extras should not become an accidental obstacle to enjoying the narrative. Buy the novel under Christie's name and distinguish it from study material, critical essays or a theatrical adaptation.

How should you choose an edition and a second book?

Before ordering, confirm the author, full title, language and format. For Doyle, inspect a collection's contents rather than assuming every book with “Sherlock Holmes” on the cover is the same selection. For either author, sample the page layout if you care about font size, annotations or illustrations. Page count alone does not measure completeness because typesetting varies.

Try one book first. If you enjoy Watson's voice but want a longer plot, move from Adventures to Hound. If you enjoy Poirot's household investigation, try Ackroyd; if you want a change to an isolated ensemble, choose And Then There Were None. These are reader-fit paths rather than a contest with one final winner.
