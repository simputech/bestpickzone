The Best Historical Fiction Books — one for every kind of reader

Start with All the Light We Cannot See for intersecting wartime lives, Girl with a Pearl Earring for an artist’s household, or Hamnet for a family story shaped by grief. The eight recommendations below serve different reading moods.

Choosing historical fiction by setting alone can lead to the wrong book. Two novels set during the same war can differ greatly in narration, emotional intensity and scale. This guide separates domestic stories, journeys, court drama and formally inventive novels so that you can choose the reading experience you actually want. These are editorial recommendations, not a claim that one novel is best for every reader.

Best overall · read this one

All the Light We Cannot See

Anthony Doerr

Occupied France, WWII

Anthony Doerr’s novel brings together Marie-Laure, a blind French girl, and Werner, a German boy with a gift for radio technology, during World War II. The publisher’s synopsis explains those parallel lives. Choose it when you want an expansive wartime setting built around particular people rather than a military campaign account.

The 2015 Pulitzer citation identifies its short chapters and interest in technology’s contradictory power. Our recommendation is to sample several chapters before deciding: the changing viewpoints are part of the attraction, but a reader who wants to stay continuously with one narrator may prefer another choice here. A prize establishes recognition, not a guarantee of your response.

Skip this if: very short, fragmented chapters annoy you — the book is built almost entirely out of them.

Best for book clubs

The Book Thief

Markus Zusak

Nazi Germany, WWII

Markus Zusak’s novel follows Liesel, a foster child in Nazi Germany, whose attachment to books develops while her family shelters a Jewish man. The publisher’s description establishes the unusual narrator: Death. That voice is a major reason to try a sample instead of treating this as a conventional wartime coming-of-age story.

We recommend it for a group interested in discussing who tells a story, what is revealed in advance and how reading connects people. It can pair usefully with All the Light We Cannot See, but the narration creates a distinct experience. Do not choose it as a light comfort read simply because books and a young protagonist are central to its premise.

Skip this if: the idea of Death as narrator does not appeal to you; try a sample before committing.

For family and grief

Hamnet

Maggie O'Farrell

1590s England

Maggie O’Farrell imagines the family life surrounding Shakespeare’s son Hamnet and his death at eleven. The publisher’s synopsis foregrounds Agnes, her marriage and her children. Choose this for an intimate family-centered approach to the past, rather than an account of theatrical achievements or a biography of Shakespeare.

Our recommendation is strongest for readers who want to stay with domestic relationships and grief. A famous name supplies context, but the family is the reason to read. The invented interior lives should be understood as fiction rather than evidence about what the historical people thought. Consider the central child-loss theme before choosing it for someone dealing with a recent bereavement.

Skip this if: you want plot and momentum — this is a quiet, atmospheric, grief-centered book by design.

4
Best bold experiment

The Underground Railroad

Colson Whitehead

Antebellum South

Colson Whitehead follows Cora’s escape from enslavement in Georgia. The novel makes the Underground Railroad a physical railway, a deliberate imaginative departure described in the publisher’s account reproduced by the Pulitzer organization. This is the choice here for a reader interested in how an invented form can examine historical violence.

Approach it with room for both the immediate pursuit and the different worlds Cora encounters. We would not give it to someone specifically requesting strict documentary realism. Its treatment of enslavement is central, not incidental background to an adventure. Read the novel as an artistic construction and use historical nonfiction separately when you want to investigate the real networks and events.

Skip this if: you want strict historical realism, or you're not up for unflinching depictions of slavery's brutality — both are central here.

5
Best odyssey

Cold Mountain

Charles Frazier

Civil War Appalachia

Charles Frazier’s novel follows Inman, a wounded Confederate deserter, on his journey toward home, while Ada tries to sustain a farm. The National Book Foundation’s page verifies the 1997 Fiction award and describes those intertwined paths. Choose it when a journey and the effort of survival interest you more than battlefield strategy.

The contrast between traveling and staying gives you two ways to enter the story. We recommend it for readers who want the landscape and ordinary work to matter alongside the hope of reunion. If your immediate wish is a sequence of tactical battles, this is a different kind of war novel. A film adaptation is also a separate experience; check that you are buying Frazier’s prose book.

Skip this if: you want a fast read — the prose is rich and the pacing is deliberately meditative.

For a contained setting

Girl with a Pearl Earring

Tracy Chevalier

1660s Delft

Tracy Chevalier imagines Griet, a maid in Vermeer’s household, being drawn into the painter’s work. On her own book page, Chevalier explains that the model’s unknown identity gave her room to invent. Choose this for the tensions of an artist’s home and studio, not a solved historical mystery about the painting.

Its contained setting is our reason to suggest it when you do not want a multigenerational epic. Work, household position and attention become the practical pressures around Griet. You need not be an art specialist to try it, but an interest in looking closely is useful. Page counts vary by edition, so compare the actual format rather than relying on a universal weekend-reading promise.

Skip this if: you're after scope and scale — this one is deliberately small and contained.

For Tudor court drama

The Other Boleyn Girl

Philippa Gregory

Tudor court, 1520s

Philippa Gregory places Mary Boleyn’s perspective at the center of Tudor court drama. Her official book page identifies the opening in 1521 and discusses her choice of a less prominent historical viewpoint. Choose it when sisters, ambition and access to royal power are the elements that draw you into historical fiction.

Our suggestion is to read it for the relationships and stakes of the novel, then pursue a biography if you want to separate its choices from documented history. Fiction can create conversations and motives that a historical record cannot supply. You do not need to dismiss that invention to enjoy it, but it is a different contract from reading a scholarly account.

Skip this if: you are looking for a nonfiction biography or a documented account of Tudor events.

8
For memory and haunting

Beloved

Toni Morrison

Post-war Ohio, 1870s

Toni Morrison’s novel follows Sethe after her escape from enslavement, with a haunted home and a past that refuses to remain past. The National Endowment for the Arts reading resource explains the nonlinear structure and the historical Margaret Garner case that helped inspire it. Choose it for an emotionally demanding exploration of memory, survival and family.

We recommend allowing time to revisit passages as the narrative gives you more context. There is no need to “graduate” through easier books before you are permitted to read Morrison; willingness to attend to the voice is more relevant than a reading checklist. The depictions of enslavement, trauma and child death are substantial reasons to consider your current reading capacity.

Skip this if: you want a linear, emotionally light story right now. The structure and painful material are central to this novel.
