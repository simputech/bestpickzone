Why is the perforated metal peel the simplest first purchase?

The Ooni 12-inch perforated peel combines a broad support surface with a thin leading edge for getting underneath a baked pizza. Its holes provide an exit for loose flour. That gives it two recurring jobs during a cook and makes it a more complete starting tool than a small turning peel.

Perforations do not make damp, over-topped dough incapable of sticking. Stretch on a preparation surface, move to a lightly floured peel, top promptly, and check that the base can move before approaching the oven. A stuck section should be corrected while you can still work safely at the preparation area. Do not try to solve it by holding the peel deep in a hot oven and shaking harder.

For successive pizzas, separate the hot retrieval job from the next raw base. A warm peel can change how dough behaves, and a crowded preparation surface makes that harder to manage. Let the tool cool as appropriate before loading again, or use a second launching surface if your group genuinely works in parallel. That is a reason to add a board after a few sessions, not proof every owner needs three peels immediately.

  • Pros: documented Koda 12 compatibility; launches and retrieves; perforations shed excess flour; one tool covers the essential transfer jobs.
  • Cons: raw dough still needs preparation; the broad blade leaves less maneuvering space than a turning head; it cannot stay loaded with the next pizza while retrieving the current one.

Skip this if you already have a sound 12-inch metal peel that fits and launches reliably. Holes alone are not a compelling reason to replace a working tool.

When is a 7-inch turning peel worth adding?

The compact Ooni turning peel is for rotating a pizza after its base has set enough to move. Its smaller head leaves room to maneuver inside a small oven. It is our optional second purchase for someone who finds turning awkward, rather than the first purchase for launching raw dough.

A turning peel supports only part of a full pizza. That lets you work under an edge and reposition the pizza without putting a full-width blade underneath every time. It also requires learning where the head is and how the crust responds. A first attempt at turning a soft, just-launched base may tear or fold it; a smaller tool cannot compensate for trying to move the pizza too early.

Ooni now offers 7-inch and 9-inch turning choices. Select the compact 7-inch version for the recommendation here, and check the listing's size selector rather than relying on the first photograph. Older product descriptions may quote different rounded dimensions. We have not verified identical shaft lengths across generations and do not transfer an old catalog length to a current variant.

  • Pros: more space around the head for rotation; keeps the turning task inside the oven; leaves a launching peel available for another base.
  • Cons: extra storage and expense; requires practice; does not support an uncooked 12-inch pizza like a full launching blade.

Should you launch from bamboo instead of metal?

The 12-inch Ooni bamboo peel makes sense as a dedicated preparation and launching surface, especially when another person tops the next pizza while you cook. It also serves pizza outside the oven. Its thicker wooden edge makes it a less useful choice for slipping underneath a cooked base.

Wood and metal ask for slightly different habits. A board is a pleasant surface for staging a raw pizza, but it still needs appropriate flour and prompt launching. Sauce spills and wet toppings can defeat either material. Keep a board used for raw dough separate from a finished-pizza cutting surface when necessary, or clean and dry it before changing roles. A wet washed board is not ready for the next launch.

For one cook making one pizza at a time, a perforated metal peel alone is a reasonable starting kit. For a busy family session, bamboo plus metal creates a useful division: one cool launching surface and one retrieval tool. Bamboo plus a small turning peel leaves you without a broad metal retrieval blade; do not choose that combination just to minimize the item count.

Skip this if storage is tight or you already have a preparation routine that works with metal. Ooni says its bamboo board is not intended for prolonged extreme heat exposure. Use it briefly for its transfer job and follow the care instructions; do not leave it in the oven like a tray.

How much space do the handles need?

Check the whole movement in your actual cooking area, not only the blade's ability to enter the oven. You need to approach the mouth, withdraw the pizza, and turn toward the landing surface without hitting a wall, railing, chair, or another person. An extra-long handle can be awkward on a small patio.

With the oven off and cold, rehearse the path using the intended tool or a safely held outline of its published full length. Include your hand position and the width of a carried pizza. This is a placement check, not a new minimum clearance specification: retain Ooni's required clearances around the oven itself. A shorter board does not make an otherwise unsafe oven location acceptable.

For storage, compare the full 659 mm metal-peel length and 505 mm bamboo-board length with a drawer or hanging location. Add space so the leading edge is protected. Ooni calls for hand washing and indoor storage of the perforated peel; avoid abrasive cleaning. Let the bamboo board dry before putting it away. Buying a tool you cannot store flat or protect between sessions is a false economy.

Which purchases can Koda 12 owners skip?

Skip 14- or 16-inch launching peels intended for larger ovens, a second turning peel in another diameter, and giant restaurant handles unless your layout specifically needs the reach. Do not buy Koda 2 accessories on the assumption that “2” means a newer version of the same 12-inch dimensions. Oven identity and the selected accessory size must both match.

Start here: one 12-inch metal launching/retrieval peel. Add a compact turning peel for rotation, then a bamboo board only if parallel preparation makes it useful.
