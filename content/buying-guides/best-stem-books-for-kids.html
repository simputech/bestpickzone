Ada Twist, Scientist — Andrea Beaty

Andrea Beaty’s text and David Roberts’s illustrations follow Ada’s questioning and attempts to investigate the world around her. The picture-book format supports stopping to notice visual details, rather than requiring a child to decode a long passage independently. Choose this when a shared story is the easiest way to begin talking about why something happens.

The original picture book is different from the chapter books, project books and television-related titles that also use Ada’s name. Confirm Beaty and Roberts on the edition you select. A child can enjoy the character without completing an activity, and a parent need not turn every page into a lesson. If a question sparks interest, let the child describe what they noticed before offering an explanation.

Choose it for: a rhyming read-aloud about curiosity. Skip it if: you want a step-by-step experiment manual.

Rosie Revere, Engineer — Andrea Beaty

Beaty and illustrator David Roberts introduce a child who builds inventions and hesitates to show them. Her great-great-aunt Rose encourages her to keep trying. This pairs naturally with Ada when a reader is more interested in building something than explaining a mysterious observation, but either story can appeal to children of any gender.

The publisher describes the adult relative as Rose, not another child named Rosie. Small character details matter when choosing or discussing a book. Separate the picture book from Rosie Revere and the Raucous Riveters, a chapter-book title, and from the associated project book. If you want actual activities, inspect that different product rather than expecting the storybook to contain a complete project course.

Choose it for: a story about making things and responding to setbacks. Skip it if: you want measured engineering exercises or instructions for a working machine.

Hidden Figures Young Readers’ Edition — Margot Lee Shetterly

Shetterly’s work brings attention to women whose mathematical work contributed to American aeronautics and the space program. The history includes racial segregation and barriers to professional opportunity as well as the work itself. Choose the young readers edition when a reader wants a sustained account of real lives rather than a single invented character’s experiment.

The adult book, young readers edition, picture-book adaptation and film are distinct versions. Check the full subtitle and a sample: the shared words Hidden Figures do not establish equivalent length or content. We do not assign a universal independent-reading age from the title alone. An adult can help with historical context without making the reader feel that every unfamiliar term is a failure.

Choose it for: historical nonfiction about Black women in mathematics and aerospace. Skip it if: you want a brief fictional picture-book read-aloud.

Megan Stine’s biography, illustrated by Ted Hammond, introduces Curie through her life and scientific work. This is a different route from the fictional inventors in the Questioneers books: the reader follows a historical person rather than a story designed around one imaginative problem. Choose it when the child asks who made a discovery and what obstacles that person faced.

Match the book to an actual reading situation

For a child who enjoys being read to but stops at long blocks of text, compare the Ada and Rosie picture books. Let the child choose between questioning and inventing as a story premise. Older children can still enjoy an illustrated story; format is a preference and access question, not a test of maturity. Avoid presenting a picture book as something they must outgrow on a fixed birthday.

For a reader who already likes biographies, show a sample from Who Was Marie Curie? and the young readers Hidden Figures. The first follows one life; the second opens a broader historical setting and several careers. For a child who jumps from animals to machines to everyday objects, the specific National Geographic browsing book may fit better than requiring one continuous narrative.

A useful follow-up without promising learning outcomes

After Ada, ask what question the character is trying to answer. After Rosie, ask what she changes when an invention does not work. After a biography, distinguish a detail about the person’s life from a claim about the science. These are optional conversation prompts, not a tested curriculum or a guarantee of stronger problem-solving skills.

If a book suggests an activity, read the complete directions first, including adult-supervision requirements. Choose a suitable workspace and materials rather than improvising around heat, electricity or chemicals because an illustrated character makes experimentation look easy. The shortlist is for selecting books; it does not certify every project printed in a related activity edition. A question, drawing or discussion can be a satisfying follow-up without building anything at all.
