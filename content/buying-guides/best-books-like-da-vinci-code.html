For another historical puzzle-and-chase premise after The Da Vinci Code, try Steve Berry's The Templar Legacy, the first Cotton Malone novel. The Historian adds a supernatural strand. The best match depends on whether you want pursuit, scholarship, atmosphere or a connection across time.

The Templar Legacy: the Cotton Malone starting point

Berry's official page explicitly labels The Templar Legacy Cotton Malone Series Book 1. Malone is a former US Justice Department operative working as an antiquarian bookseller in Copenhagen when his former supervisor draws him into danger connected to centuries-old clues.

That combination makes it our first comparison for readers who want historical puzzles combined with a present-day thriller. The operative background changes the balance from a primarily academic protagonist. If you want discoveries followed by external threats and pursuit, the premise is a closer fit than selecting a novel solely because its author discusses medieval history.

The author's anniversary note confirms that the series began in 2006. Start with one book before buying a collection. A long-running catalog gives you continuation options, but it does not justify promising identical quality or a particular reading speed across every installment.

Historical references are ingredients in the fiction. If a claim about the Templars interests you beyond the story, check a historical source rather than carrying the novel's conspiracy into a factual account. A plausible puzzle is not proof that a hidden history is real.

The Name of the Rose: investigation inside the historical setting

The publisher's record describes Brother William's investigation of deaths at a medieval abbey. Unlike a modern chase built around old clues, this story places the investigator within the historical world being examined. The linked English edition credits translator William Weaver.

Choose it if interpreting texts, arguments and institutional tensions is the attraction. The main tradeoff is the amount of attention you want to give that setting and its ideas. Sample the narration and dialogue rather than accepting a promise that the opening becomes easy after a fixed number of pages.

A reader seeking a quick succession of modern locations may find the abbey's concentrated world less aligned with the desired experience. A reader interested in how interpretation itself becomes part of an investigation has a concrete reason to try it.

Check the translator and any introduction when comparing editions. Introductory essays can discuss later events, so consider reading them afterward if you prefer to avoid spoilers. Page counts vary with edition design and additional material; do not assume the shortest listing contains the same apparatus.

Labyrinth: two women, two periods

Hachette's synopsis places Alaïs in Carcassonne in 1209 and Alice in a 2005 archaeological discovery. A book said to concern the Grail links the historical and later strands. Choose it when following a connection between periods matters more than staying beside one investigator throughout.

Keep track of which timeline you are in and what each character can know. That simple distinction preserves the mystery without needing an elaborate chart. If you dislike switching away from a storyline just as it becomes interesting, sample the transitions before committing to the full novel.

Mosse's publisher reading-order guide separates her different series. Use a named sequence if you want to continue rather than buying every book by the author as though it followed these same characters. Historical settings and recurring regional interests do not automatically make all titles direct sequels.

The medieval setting does not guarantee historical accuracy for every invented event. Read it as fiction, with the historical background supplying context rather than authentication of the Grail story. The reader's interest in two women acting across time is enough to distinguish this recommendation.

The Historian: research with a supernatural boundary

The publisher describes a young woman's discovery that her family's past connects to Vlad the Impaler and a threat extending across time. Choose it for the combination of inquiry, family secrets and vampire lore.

The supernatural premise is an important difference from a thriller that asks readers to believe a conspiracy could exist within ordinary reality. If you want strictly non-supernatural suspense, choose another option. If tracing documents and inherited questions is the appeal, this offers a different kind of investigative journey.

Sample for atmosphere and the way information is relayed. Consider whether you want to spend time with the documents and recollections through which the mystery develops, or would prefer the more direct pursuit premise of The Templar Legacy.

References to historical figures do not make vampire lore historical evidence. If the book sparks interest in a place or period, follow that interest into nonfiction separately. The distinction lets you enjoy the imaginative premise without presenting its explanations as established fact.

The Rule of Four: a textual puzzle at a smaller scale

The publisher's description places the story at Princeton, where a difficult Renaissance text draws students into a dangerous mystery. Both Ian Caldwell and Dustin Thomason are credited as authors. Choose it if academic investigation is the feature you want to retain while changing the setting.

The campus focus brings relationships and competing commitments close to the puzzle. If you mainly want international travel and large action sequences, the premise may feel more contained than expected. If you want the act of decoding to affect friendships and a student's future, that containment is a reason to consider it.

The use of a real old book does not validate the novel's fictional solution or every historical assertion. Keep the object, its documented history and the invented plot distinct. A reader can be intrigued by all three without treating them as interchangeable.

The Amber Room: a separate lost-art option

Berry's synopsis follows Rachel Cutler and Paul in a search connected to her father's death and the missing Amber Room. This is a separate recommendation from The Templar Legacy. Select it if the lost-art premise is more appealing than the beginning of Cotton Malone's series.

Check the title and character description in a bundle before buying it as a series introduction. An author can write both standalone work and recurring-character novels; a shared thriller style does not make one title the first installment of another. That distinction is more useful than assigning an unsupported “closest pacing match” badge.

If you mainly want more Robert Langdon

Dan Brown's official catalog lists Angels & Demons, The Da Vinci Code, The Lost Symbol, Inferno, Origin and The Secret of Secrets in the Langdon series. Compare the premises there if the recurring protagonist is the attraction. We do not call their structures identical or require you to finish them before trying another author.
