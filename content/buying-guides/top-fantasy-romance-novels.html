Choose the kind of romantic fantasy you want before choosing the most familiar cover. For dragon-rider training, consider Fourth Wing. For a marriage alliance between unfamiliar peoples, consider Radiance. For magical letters between rival journalists, try Divine Rivals. These are different starting points, not interchangeable versions of one “best” book.

The fifteen selections below retain a broad view of fantasy love stories. Some are adult romantic fantasy; others are young adult fantasy or mythological fiction. In particular, The Song of Achilles should not be bought expecting a conventional happy ending. Rites of the Starling is a sequel, so its 2026 release does not make it a suitable entry point.

Make three choices first

  • Premise: choose between court politics, a quest, a deadly contest, letters or a marriage alliance. A shared trope does not mean the books spend the same amount of time on romance.
  • Commitment: distinguish a first volume from a continuation. A satisfying premise can still lead into further books; buy or borrow an opening volume before committing to a large set.
  • Content: decide which themes you want to avoid. Author warnings and a sample are more useful than an unexplained “spice” score. Young adult, adult and “romantasy” labels are not complete content descriptions.

Our recommendations below are editorial matches to documented premises and series information. They do not imply first-hand testing, a survey of reader outcomes or a ranking established by sales. Amazon links open searches; select the author, language and format yourself before purchasing.

1. A Court of Thorns and Roses — Sarah J. Maas

Fae courts and a series starting point. Feyre kills a wolf and is taken into the realm of the fae. Bloomsbury identifies this as the opening novel in the series. Choose it if the movement from a human life into a dangerous faerie world is the attraction.

Begin with this title rather than a later volume selected for its cover. The recommendation is about that premise, not a claim that every newcomer will prefer it. If you want a single book with no further series commitment, make that a separate requirement when choosing.

2. Fourth Wing — Rebecca Yarros

Dragon riders and military-school danger. Violet Sorrengail enters a war college for dragon riders instead of the scholarly life she expected. Yarros identifies it as Empyrean book one. This is the clear match here for readers specifically seeking dragons, training and dangerous competition alongside romance.

The author explicitly flags intense violence, injuries, death and on-page sexual activity. A school setting does not make this a children’s book. Sample the novel and consult those warnings if either combat or sexual detail is a deciding factor; a dragon on the cover tells you neither.

3. The Serpent and the Wings of Night — Carissa Broadbent

Vampire politics and a deadly contest. Oraya is a human raised by a vampire king who enters the Kejari tournament and makes a dangerous alliance with Raihn. The author’s synopsis makes the contest and unequal power between humans and vampires central to the setup.

Choose this for a romantic alliance formed under mortal pressure. Broadbent’s content notes include violence, abuse-related material and sexual content; consult the full list rather than treating “dark” as a precise warning. It is a series entry, not a promise that every conflict ends in one volume.

4. From Blood and Ash — Jennifer L. Armentrout

Forbidden attraction and a prescribed role. Poppy’s role as the Maiden restricts her choices; the arrival of guard Hawke draws duty and desire into conflict. The author labels this book one of Blood and Ash. Choose it when questioning religious and political authority is part of the romantic premise you want.

Armentrout supplies a separate reading order for Blood and Ash and the companion Flesh and Fire books. Use that if you continue: a companion prequel is not automatically the best place for a first-time reader to start. Do not buy an entire mixed-series bundle before checking its contents.

5. Radiance — Grace Draven

A marriage alliance across different peoples. Draven’s Wraith Kings page introduces Brishen, a Kai prince, and Ildiko, a human noblewoman, whose marriage seals a political and trade alliance. That starting arrangement makes it a useful alternative to a tournament or military-school story.

Consider it if learning to understand an unfamiliar spouse interests you more than competitive trials. That is a comparison of the premises, not a psychological judgment that one fictional relationship is healthier than all the others. The author lists Radiance as book one, with additional Wraith Kings stories following.

Author or publisher details for Radiance. Search Amazon for Radiance editions.

6. The Bridge Kingdom — Danielle L. Jensen

Marriage, espionage and divided loyalty. Jensen’s series page groups the opening story around Lara and Aren. For readers drawn to a politically consequential relationship, this is an alternative to choosing primarily for dragons or an intricate magical system.

Start with The Bridge Kingdom and follow with The Traitor Queen for the opening pair’s story. The wider series changes its central couples, so “the next book” and “more of exactly the same relationship” are not interchangeable. Check the author’s grouped reading order before selecting later installments.

7. Once Upon a Broken Heart — Stephanie Garber

A dangerous fairy-tale bargain. Evangeline Fox makes a bargain with Jacks, the Prince of Hearts, to stop a wedding. The promised payment is three kisses at times and places he chooses. Choose it if bargains, curses and uncertain wishes are more appealing than a military campaign.

Macmillan publishes it as young adult fiction. The opening novel is followed by The Ballad of Never After and A Curse for True Love; the publisher also lists a later companion, The Mirror of Infinite Endings. A three-book boxed set therefore does not necessarily include every related publication.

8. Kingdom of the Wicked — Kerri Maniscalco

A murder investigation involving witches and demons. Emilia’s search after her twin sister’s murder brings her into contact with Wrath, one of the princes of Hell. The publisher’s premise combines an investigation with attraction to a potentially dangerous ally.

Choose it for that mystery-and-demons combination. The murder is part of the setup, so this is not an appropriate recommendation for someone specifically avoiding bereavement or violence. Check individual volumes before gifting a series: the presence of romance alone does not establish a uniform level of sexual or violent detail.

Author or publisher details for Kingdom of the Wicked. Search Amazon for Kingdom of the Wicked editions.

9. The Song of Achilles — Madeline Miller

A mythological love story with tragedy. Miller retells the connection between Patroclus and Achilles against the approach of the Trojan War. It belongs on this broader list for readers seeking a love story within myth, rather than because it follows every convention of commercial genre romance.

Choose it only with the expectation of tragedy, not a guaranteed happy ending. That distinction matters more than whether a shop places it near fantasy romance. It is also a retelling, not a translation of Homer; readers seeking the ancient poem should choose an edition of the Iliad separately.

Author or publisher details for The Song of Achilles. Search Amazon for The Song of Achilles editions.

10. The Cruel Prince — Holly Black

Court ambition and a hostile faerie world. Black’s synopsis introduces Jude, a mortal taken with her sisters into the High Court of Faerie after their parents are murdered. Her wish to belong and gain power brings her into conflict with Prince Cardan.

Choose it when political maneuvering is an attraction in its own right. Someone seeking a gentle courtship should inspect the opening and premise first: cruelty is not merely a decorative title. The young-reader publisher designation is a market category, not a guarantee of suitability for every younger reader.

Author or publisher details for The Cruel Prince. Search Amazon for The Cruel Prince editions.

11. These Hollow Vows — Lexi Ryan

A rescue mission between rival faerie courts. Brie attempts to rescue her sister by stealing magical relics from one faerie court for another. Ryan’s synopsis places Prince Ronan and the Unseelie leader Finn on different sides of her divided trust and attraction.

This is a useful choice if a love triangle is something you actively want. If triangles frustrate you, that is a reason to select another premise rather than hope the setup disappears. Ryan lists These Twisted Bonds as the second book of the duology; begin with These Hollow Vows.

12. One Dark Window — Rachel Gillig

Gothic magic and a threatening inner presence. Elspeth shares her mind with the Nightmare, an ancient spirit whose protection has a cost. The quest involving Providence Cards places the relationship within a problem of dangerous magic and secrecy.

Consider this if atmosphere and the rules of a curse matter as much to you as romantic attraction. If an invasive supernatural presence is a theme you avoid, sample carefully. A label such as “low spice” would not tell you whether its darker imagery suits you, so this guide does not use an unsupported numerical heat rating.

Author or publisher details for One Dark Window. Search Amazon for One Dark Window editions.

13. Divine Rivals — Rebecca Ross

Rival journalists and magical correspondence. Iris Winnow’s letters unexpectedly reach Roman Kitt, her rival at the newspaper, while a war among gods draws closer. The publisher identifies this as the first Letters of Enchantment novel.

Choose it for the correspondence and workplace-rivalry setup. War, a missing brother and a mother’s addiction are part of the publisher’s description, so “romantic letters” should not be read as “free of difficult subjects.” Ruthless Vows continues and concludes Iris and Roman’s story; this opening volume is not the whole arc.

14. When the Moon Hatched — Sarah A. Parker

Rebellion, dragons and hidden history. The publisher’s synopsis follows assassin Raeve, her imprisonment by powerful fae and her encounter with dragon rider Kaan Vaegor. Choose it when the combination of political danger, a dragon rider and unresolved personal history attracts you.

This is a different premise from Fourth Wing’s college training, even though both involve dragons. Read a sample before choosing on that shared feature alone. Check the listed format and series position rather than assuming a deluxe cover, new printing or retailer bundle represents a new standalone story.

15. Rites of the Starling — Devney Perry

A continuation for Shield of Sparrows readers. The author labels this Shield of Sparrows book two. Its princess’s journey through a cursed realm builds on separation, family secrets and dangers established in the earlier story. It belongs here as a continuation option, not as the default first book for a newcomer.

Start with Shield of Sparrows if you have not read it. Perry’s newsletter archive dates the sequel’s release announcement April 7, 2026. That verifies a release date, not a claim that it is the year’s best book or that a particular retailer has stock today.

Compare the edition, not just the title

Search results can mix novels, adaptations, summaries, annotated editions and collectible printings. Confirm the author and language first, then check whether the listing is a single book or a set. For audio, inspect the narrator and whether the recording is abridged or dramatized. An attractive sprayed-edge edition is not evidence that the text includes every bonus scene.

Series catalogs also change. Use Armentrout’s combined reading order, Ryan’s publication list and Perry’s release archive for the distinctions above. Macmillan identifies The Mirror of Infinite Endings as a September 22, 2026 companion and Ruthless Vows as the conclusion to Divine Rivals.
