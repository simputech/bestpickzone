For a twelve-year-old choosing a summer book, try Holes for a mystery with connections to uncover, Hatchet for a solitary survival story, or The Lightning Thief for a mythology-based quest. The Crossover combines basketball and family relationships in verse, while The Wild Robot follows a machine adapting to an island. These choices are open to readers of any gender; the useful distinction is what the individual wants to read.

Holes: follow the connections

The publisher's synopsis places Stanley Yelnats at Camp Green Lake, where boys must dig holes and Stanley begins to suspect another purpose behind the work. The selected publisher record recommends ages ten and up. Choose it when the reader enjoys noticing clues and watching separate pieces of a story connect.

The camp is a place of punishment and unfairness, not a pleasant summer-camp setting. That distinction matters if the title or a familiar movie image suggests a light outdoor adventure. A reader who wants a break from stories about mistreatment may prefer a different premise, even if the mystery sounds appealing.

For an optional reading activity, keep a small list of recurring names, objects and places. Add a question when something seems connected rather than searching for a plot explanation online. This preserves the pleasure of discovery. A parent can discuss what Stanley understands at a particular point without revealing how the threads resolve.

There is no need to sell the book as secretly educational or promise it will turn a reluctant reader into an enthusiastic one. Its concrete attraction is the puzzle. If the opening does not create curiosity, try another option instead of treating completion as proof of the child's ability.

Hatchet: a story built around being alone

Simon & Schuster describes thirteen-year-old Brian traveling to visit his father after his parents' divorce. A plane crash leaves him in the Canadian wilderness with a hatchet. Choose it for a reader interested in how one character responds to repeated practical problems and fear.

The isolation distinguishes it from a team adventure. If conversation between friends is the reader's favorite part of a book, the premise may feel less appealing than Percy Jackson's quest. If observing a character's changing decisions is the attraction, the limited company is part of the reason to try it.

The crash and Brian's family situation deserve a content check. A survival novel can also make dangerous actions seem simpler than they are. Treat it as fiction, not instructions for eating wild plants, handling a tool or managing an outdoor emergency. Interest in the story does not require copying Brian's choices.

A useful discussion is how Brian revises an assumption after something goes wrong. That keeps attention on the narrative's problem-solving without claiming the book teaches verified survival skills. For an outdoor-interested reader, compare the character's emotional response with the practical situation, rather than ranking every action as a model to imitate.

The Lightning Thief: a clear place to begin a series

Rick Riordan's official page identifies this as book one of Percy Jackson and the Olympians. Percy discovers a connection to the Greek gods and goes on a quest involving Zeus's stolen lightning bolt. Choose it if companions, mythological encounters and the possibility of more books are the attraction.

Beginning with the first novel is useful for meeting the characters and world in their introductory context. It does not obligate the reader to buy a complete set. Try the opening book before committing to a bundle, especially if mythology is a new interest rather than an established preference.

Riordan's graphic-novel page identifies a separate adaptation. That may be a good choice for a reader who wants sequential art, but it is not the same format as the original prose novel. For school reading, check whether the adaptation satisfies the assignment. For personal reading, let the format preference be part of the decision.

Modern retellings adapt myths to their own story. A reader curious about an encountered god or creature can compare a separate mythology source afterward without needing to memorize background before starting. Fantasy danger and conflict are part of the appeal, so this is not a promise of a gentle adventure for every child.

The Crossover: basketball and family in verse

Andersen Press describes twelve-year-old twins Josh and Jordan, their basketball ambitions and challenges to their relationship. The novel is written in verse. Choose it when the reader wants sports alongside a family story, or wants to try a different page layout and rhythm from prose.

More open space on a page does not mean the story is emotionally light or that every reader will finish quickly. The family's difficulties matter alongside the games. If illness or loss is a subject the reader wants to avoid, review fuller content information before choosing rather than relying on the sports cover.

Try a poem silently and then aloud, noticing how spacing, repeated sounds or pauses affect the feeling of movement. This can be a private experiment; the child need not perform for an audience. The aim is to see whether the form is enjoyable, not to prove that poetry is easier than a conventional novel.

There is also a graphic adaptation illustrated by Dawud Anyabwile. Match the version to the intended experience. A search result with a different page total or cover can be another legitimate format, so check the description instead of assuming every listing contains the same presentation.

The Wild Robot: a machine meeting a living environment

Hachette's description introduces Roz, a robot who wakes alone on a wild island and must adapt to her surroundings. Peter Brown's illustrated novel is a candidate for readers interested in animals, technology and the question of how an unfamiliar being finds a place.

The story combines those interests through fiction. It should not be presented as a factual guide to animal behavior or robot engineering. If the reader enjoys asking what a machine can learn, the premise gives that curiosity a narrative setting without requiring technical knowledge.

It can be read independently or shared aloud. Choose based on how the reader wants to experience the book, not an assumption that shared reading is only for younger children. The publisher's book-club guide provides questions for discussion, but includes details from later in the story.

Do not assume an animal-and-robot story contains no danger or sadness. If a film prompted the interest, verify that the result is the original novel rather than a picture-book adaptation or a movie-related activity book. Familiar characters can appear in several products with different reading demands.

Turn the shortlist into one useful choice

Compare two openings with different structures: Hatchet against The Lightning Thief for solitude versus companions, or Holes against The Crossover for prose mystery versus verse. Ask which story the reader wants to return to. That is more concrete than asking whether he is a “good reader.”

Check the school's requirements if the book is assigned. Otherwise, a library copy, sample or audio preview can help settle the choice. Page totals vary by edition and do not establish reading time.
