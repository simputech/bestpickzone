Choose All Quiet on the Western Front for a German soldier’s fictional perspective on the First World War, The Things They Carried for linked stories that question how war is narrated, or Catch-22 for satire about institutional power. A Farewell to Arms puts a relationship inside wartime uncertainty, while Matterhorn follows a Marine unit in Vietnam. These novels differ in form as well as setting. None should be promoted as the single most accurate account of combat or as a substitute for historical evidence.

All Quiet on the Western Front — Erich Maria Remarque

Paul Bäumer’s experience places the reader among young German soldiers rather than above the conflict with a map of campaigns. Training, fear, comradeship and the distance from civilian life shape the perspective. Choose it when the effect of war on a person interests you more than a sequence of commanders’ decisions.

English editions use different translations. A 2026 Penguin edition credits Maria Tatar, while older translations remain available. Compare a sample and record the translator if buying for a group. The title also belongs to screen adaptations; a familiar cover image does not establish that a book includes the same scenes or ending as a film. The novel’s violence and losses remain substantial despite its relative brevity.

Choose it for: a soldier-centered First World War novel. Skip it if: you want strategic overview or a triumphal military adventure.

The Things They Carried — Tim O’Brien

O’Brien served in Vietnam, but the book’s narrator and remembered events are part of a literary construction. The National Endowment for the Arts describes its deliberate movement between fact and fiction. Choose it if you want to ask what a narrator is trying to convey and why a story might be retold with changed emphasis.

His military service does not make every scene autobiographical. Do not quote a scene as a documented event from his life without separate evidence. Read the pieces together for their connections, even though the linked-story structure offers natural places to pause and revisit a voice or image.

Choose it for: linked Vietnam stories that examine memory and storytelling. Skip it if: you need each episode to function as verified autobiography.

Catch-22 — Joseph Heller

Yossarian’s attempts to avoid further bombing missions collide with rules that seem designed to defeat any reasonable escape. The shifting chronology and recurring situations are central to how the satire works. Choose it when you want institutions and language to be part of the conflict, rather than assuming the central question is simply whether a mission succeeds.

Comic scenes do not make this a light or reassuring book. The violence and deaths gain force as information returns in different contexts. The 50th anniversary edition includes an introduction and other supplementary material; those additions are not a different sequel. If preserving discovery matters, consider reading critical introductions after the novel because they may discuss later events.

Choose it for: satire, repetition and the absurdity of bureaucracy. Skip it if: you need a straightforward chronological combat plot.

A Farewell to Arms — Ernest Hemingway

An American ambulance driver, Frederic Henry, and an English nurse, Catherine Barkley, form a relationship within wartime disruption. The novel’s personal focus makes it a distinct choice from a unit-centered story. Choose it if the conflict between private hopes and events outside the characters’ control is the part of war fiction you want to explore.

This is fiction informed by a historical setting, not a direct transcript of Hemingway’s service or relationships. Editions can include introductions, alternate endings or other additional material, so match the contents when buying for a course. The word “romance” describes a central relationship; it should not be used here to promise the ending conventions a contemporary genre-romance buyer may expect.

Choose it for: a relationship shaped by the Italian front in the First World War. Skip it if: you want a broad campaign history or a comforting romance.

Matterhorn — Karl Marlantes

Marlantes follows young Marines whose physical demands and internal conflicts operate alongside orders from above. The unit perspective gives room for race, ambition, loyalty and the daily difficulty of operating in the field. Choose it when those relationships interest you enough to sustain a longer reading commitment, rather than because a larger book must be more complete.

Grove Atlantic identifies Marlantes as a Vietnam veteran and describes a novel developed over thirty-five years. His service does not establish a ranking of the novel’s accuracy against all other Vietnam fiction. His experience informs the context; the book remains a novel. Be prepared for graphic combat and difficult language.

Choose it for: a sustained Vietnam novel about a Marine unit and command pressures. Skip it if: you want a compact book or a purely personal memoir.

Two comparisons that clarify the purchase

For First World War reading, compare All Quiet with A Farewell to Arms. The first centers a German soldier’s experience, while the second connects the Italian front with a relationship and an ambulance driver’s perspective. Reading both can reveal different literary choices, but neither covers the entire war. Choose the narrative lens you want first, then use history to supply wider context.

For Vietnam, compare Matterhorn with The Things They Carried. Marlantes offers a sustained unit narrative; O’Brien repeatedly makes the telling of a story part of the subject. If uncertainty about factual status frustrates you, the latter may not be the right immediate choice. If that uncertainty is precisely what you want to discuss, it is a reason to select it rather than a defect to conceal.

A reading group can separate three questions

First ask what happens within the fictional world. Then ask how the narrator or structure makes that event available to the reader. Finally ask which historical questions would require evidence beyond the novel. Keeping those questions separate avoids treating a veteran novelist’s invented scene as testimony simply because the author served in the conflict.

The same distinction helps with Catch-22: a comic rule can illuminate how a character experiences power without proving that every military organization worked exactly that way. Participants can discuss the satire’s logic and its limits without turning literary interpretation into unsupported historical generalization. Agree on a stopping point, since later disclosures may change an earlier scene.

Before buying an edition

Check the author, translator where relevant, language and whether the offer is an unabridged text. A study guide, graphic adaptation or film companion can appear beside the novel in search results. For used copies, verify whether missing pages or extensive annotations would interfere with the intended use. For audio, sample the narration and confirm whether supplementary essays are included. These practical differences matter more than a generic “classic” label on the cover.
