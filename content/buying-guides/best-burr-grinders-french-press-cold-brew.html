Why choose the standard Encore for immersion brewing?

The Baratza Encore is a practical starting point when you want an understandable grinder for French press and cold brew. Its numbered stepped adjustment makes it easy to record a setting, change methods, and return to a previous starting point without navigating a complicated dosing interface.

The grounds bin is the important batch limit. Baratza's current regional specification lists a larger hopper than grounds-bin capacity, and its manual warns against exceeding the bin's fill line. That means filling the hopper is not permission to leave the grinder running until every bean is gone. Weigh the intended batch, watch the bin, and stop to empty it before grounds back up into the chute.

Do not confuse the standard Encore with Encore ESP or ESP Pro. Their names and silhouettes are related, but the adjustment systems and intended buying reasons differ. For this use case, a clear coarse-brew workflow matters more than paying for espresso-focused adjustment you will not use. If espresso is genuinely next on your plan, compare that requirement before purchasing twice.

  • Pros: easy-to-record settings; simple manual operation; a hopper workflow that suits repeated use of one coffee.
  • Cons: bin management during larger batches; no automatic mass-based dosing; exact capacity claims should be matched to the version sold.

Skip this if you want unattended large-batch grinding or assume the standard Encore is interchangeable with the ESP for fine espresso dialing.

When is the Ode Gen 2 a better match?

The Fellow Ode Gen 2 fits a household that weighs each brew's beans and wants a dedicated brewed-coffee grinder. Its published 100g grind capacity accommodates many ordinary home batches, but concentrated cold brew can exceed that amount even when the final serving volume sounds modest.

Consider your largest routine recipe before focusing on the normal morning cup. A 60g French-press batch fits inside a 100g capacity by mass, while a 180g concentrate batch needs at least two loads. That calculation does not tell you how long grinding takes or what cooling pauses the manual requires. It simply reveals an extra handling step that a product photograph cannot show.

Fellow explicitly excludes espresso from the Ode's intended use. That is a useful boundary rather than a flaw to explain away. If you want a grinder that stays on a brew setting while a separate espresso grinder handles fine adjustments, the specialization can simplify the station. If you need one grinder for both, choose a different model instead of hoping a burr upgrade changes the product's supported purpose.

  • Pros: a defined weighed-batch routine; brew-focused adjustment; clear 100g capacity for planning loads.
  • Cons: multiple loads for larger concentrates; not an espresso solution; more capability than some occasional cold-brew users need.

Skip this if you regularly grind large concentrate batches and dislike reloading, or plan to use the same grinder for true espresso preparation.

How should you calculate a cold-brew batch?

Calculate the dry coffee dose from the actual recipe before comparing grinder capacity. The volume printed on a brewing vessel does not tell you the grounds mass, and a concentrate recipe uses more coffee per unit of water than a drink brewed at serving strength. Compare like-for-like amounts.

As a planning example, a recipe using 1,000g of water at a 1:8 coffee-to-water ratio needs 125g of coffee. At 1:16 it needs 62.5g. Those are arithmetic examples, not a claim that either ratio is right for every cold-brew brewer. The first exceeds Ode's 100g stated capacity and also makes the Encore's bin limit relevant; the second is a much smaller handling job.

Ground coffee also takes up space in the brewer and retains liquid after draining. Do not assume a one-liter jar can hold one liter of water plus the full coffee dose with comfortable stirring room. Work from the brewer's instructions, and keep dilution separate from extraction in your notes. Otherwise you may blame the grind for coffee that merely received a different amount of water after brewing.

Should French press and cold brew use the coarsest setting?

Not automatically. Start with the grinder and brewer makers' guidance, then adjust for the recipe and the cup. “Coarse” is not a standardized number across grinders. A setting useful for a long immersion and one filter arrangement may be unsuitable for another brewing time or straining method.

Baratza's Encore manual gives a French-press starting setting of 28 and explicitly treats suggested settings as starting points. Record your own result rather than assuming the final number on the dial must be best. Keep coffee dose, water amount, and brewing procedure consistent while changing grind size. Otherwise a stronger or weaker cup cannot tell you which change mattered.

For French press, some sediment is connected to the filter and pouring method as well as the grinder. For cold brew, finer particles can affect how the batch drains through your chosen filter. We have not measured fines from these grinders, so this guide does not label either “sediment-free.” If clear coffee is your priority, evaluate the filtration workflow alongside the grinder rather than promising that a different burr shape removes all suspended particles.

What should you check about cleaning and long batches?

Read the model's maintenance and operating instructions before processing a large batch. Keep grounds below the bin fill line, do not leave the grinder unattended, and follow its permitted running and rest periods. A large hopper describes storage capacity, not a commercial-duty rating or permission for continuous grinding.

Unplug before the cleaning steps that require it, use the manufacturer's specified methods, and do not wash burrs merely because the grounds bin is removable. Oils and accumulated grounds can make a brewing change harder to evaluate. After cleaning or reassembly, confirm the grinder is correctly set up before concluding that the previous setting should produce an identical result.
