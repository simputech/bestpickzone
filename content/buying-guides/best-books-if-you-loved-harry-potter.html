If the part of Harry Potter you miss is friendship and a young hero discovering a hidden world, start by sampling Rick Riordan's The Lightning Thief. If you want a portal into another world, consider C. S. Lewis's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe. For dragons and a larger quest, try Christopher Paolini's Eragon. Adult readers looking for a different treatment of magical education can compare The Name of the Wind and The Magicians; readers drawn to rules-based powers and rebellion can investigate Mistborn.

These recommendations match particular story elements, not a promise that another series will reproduce Hogwarts. Audience matters: a shared magical-school premise does not make an adult novel suitable for the same reader as a children's fantasy. Start with the reader's interests and preferences, then check a sample of the specific book.

The Lightning Thief: friendship and mythological discovery

Rick Riordan's official page identifies The Lightning Thief as book one of Percy Jackson and the Olympians. Twelve-year-old Percy travels across the United States with companions on a quest involving Zeus's stolen lightning bolt. The author's page lists an audience of ages ten and up; treat that as publisher-style guidance and still consider the individual reader.

This is the most direct starting point in this selection for someone asking for young friendships and discovery rather than an adult reinterpretation of fantasy. The setting and mythology differ substantially from Rowling's world. That difference can be a benefit: a reader gets a new set of expectations instead of spending every chapter looking for equivalents to familiar characters.

Choose the original novel if that is what you intend to read. Riordan's site also lists a graphic-novel adaptation, and television-related covers can appear in searches. Matching “Lightning Thief” alone is not enough to establish the format. Begin with one book before committing to a set spanning several different series.

The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe: a portal, not another Hogwarts

The official C. S. Lewis site explains the two Narnia reading orders. The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe was published first, while chronological numbering begins with The Magician's Nephew. This guide recommends publication order as a way to encounter Narnia through the wardrobe story; it is a reading preference, not a claim that a chronologically numbered set is incorrect.

The appeal here is entry into a strange world and children's involvement in its conflict. Do not choose it expecting classes, exams or a long sequence of school years. Its Christian themes are also part of the comparison, especially when selecting a gift for a family whose reading preferences you do not know.

Check whether a listing is the single novel, an omnibus containing seven books or a boxed set. Illustrated editions can vary in size and presentation. A substantial omnibus may look economical but be less comfortable for a particular child to hold than an individual volume. Inspect the format rather than assuming a set is automatically the better buy.

Eragon: choose the dragon-rider quest

Christopher Paolini identifies Eragon as book one of the Inheritance Cycle. Its young protagonist becomes involved in a dragon-rider story and a wider conflict. The useful connection to Harry Potter is discovery of an unfamiliar role, not a close match in setting or narrative structure.

Consider it when the reader wants travel, a secondary world and a longer fantasy adventure. A reader whose favorite element was the everyday texture of school friendships may want to sample it before committing. A dragon on the cover signals a different kind of story opportunity; it does not tell you whether the pace or descriptive style will be a good fit.

Use the author's reading information to distinguish the Inheritance Cycle from later books in the broader world. A later release is not necessarily the next numbered volume to buy for a new reader. For a first purchase, the original Eragon is a clearer starting point than a bundle whose contents are not fully listed.

The Name of the Wind: an adult story about talent and reputation

Patrick Rothfuss's official bibliography identifies The Name of the Wind as Kingkiller Chronicle book one and The Wise Man's Fear as book two. Kvothe's account includes childhood, hardship, music and entry to the University. The magical education is one attraction, but the act of telling a life story is another.

Choose it if you want an adult fantasy with an extended individual perspective. Do not describe it to a young reader simply as “Harry Potter but better” or assume shared school elements establish age suitability. Sampling the narrative voice is especially useful here because the experience depends on spending considerable time with Kvothe's telling.

The checked official listings do not offer a completed three-novel main sequence. If a finished central story is essential, take that limitation into account; this is not a claim about how much of a future manuscript has been written. Related novellas and anniversary editions are separate products, not substitutes for an unpublished third main novel.

The Magicians: for adults who want fantasy expectations questioned

Lev Grossman's description explicitly presents The Magicians as a novel for adults. Quentin Coldwater enters a secret college of magic, but gaining access to the world he imagined does not simply provide the happiness and meaning he expected. That makes disillusionment part of the premise rather than an incidental darker scene.

It may suit an adult who wants to revisit childhood fantasy ideas from a more skeptical angle. It is a poor automatic gift for a child finishing Harry Potter. If sexual content, violence or difficult emotional material are important boundaries, investigate the full book and series rather than relying on a magical-school comparison as a content rating.

Grossman's official biography identifies the completed trilogy as The Magicians, The Magician King and The Magician's Land. Start with the first novel. A television tie-in cover does not mean the prose tells exactly the same version as the screen adaptation.

Mistborn: powers, planning and rebellion

Brandon Sanderson's original-trilogy guide identifies the first book as Mistborn: The Final Empire, followed by The Well of Ascension and The Hero of Ages. The premise combines a heist with epic fantasy and political conflict. It is a different recommendation from a story about attending school.

Compare it if learning how powers work and watching characters plan around those powers is what interests you. Expect a rebellion-oriented story rather than a replacement for the familiarity of Hogwarts. The original trilogy provides a completed arc, but that does not mean every book in the wider Mistborn setting is part of that three-volume set.

Search results may call the first novel simply Mistborn. Check the author, subtitle and set contents before buying. Later-era novels and related works should not be mistaken for book one merely because they share the series name.

Make the first purchase a sample of the new world

For a family read, compare one chapter together and discuss which features the reader actually wants. For an adult buying for themselves, decide whether completion, tone or a particular fantasy element matters most. A library copy or single-volume ebook can answer that question before a large boxed-set purchase.
