Use a small, repeatable buying test

List the keys you use every day: number pad, arrows, function row, navigation cluster and operating-system shortcuts. Then compare the physical layout. For example, someone entering long tables of figures may value a number pad more than extra mouse space; someone who rarely enters numbers may prefer a smaller board. Neither layout is universally more ergonomic or productive.

Next, consider noise in the room where the keyboard will be used. Switch color is not a universal standard across brands, and a linear switch is not necessarily silent. Keycaps, case, desk surface and typing force can all affect sound. Ask the people sharing the space before choosing clicky switches, and do not use a vendor recording as a calibrated comparison with another model.

Finally, verify connection behavior, required shortcuts and any saved settings while the return option is available. Try wake-from-sleep and device switching on the actual computers. Keep the keyboard positioned so you can reach the mouse and other tools comfortably; if discomfort persists, reconsider the whole workstation rather than expecting a switch change to solve it.
